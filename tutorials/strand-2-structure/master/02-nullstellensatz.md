# Hilbert's Nullstellensatz

## Explain Like I Am 7

Imagine a treasure map covered in equations.  The **shape drawn on
the map** (the set of points where every equation hits zero) and the
**list of equations** itself look like two totally different things —
geometry on one side, algebra on the other.  Hilbert's
"zero-place-theorem" is a magic dictionary that translates between
the two perfectly: every shape is described by exactly one tidy
ideal-of-equations, and vice versa.  This single dictionary is the
foundation of all algebraic geometry.

## Mental

Over an algebraically closed field $k$ (e.g., $\mathbb{C}$):

For an ideal $I \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, the **vanishing
locus** is

$$
V(I) = \{p \in k^n : f(p) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}.
$$

For a set $S \subseteq k^n$, the **vanishing ideal** is

$$
I(S) = \{f \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n] : f(p) = 0 \text{ for all } p \in S\}.
$$

**Hilbert's Nullstellensatz**:

$$
I(V(I)) = \sqrt I,
$$

where $\sqrt I = \{f : f^k \in I \text{ for some } k \ge 1\}$ is the
**radical** of $I$.

## Three flavors

- **Weak Nullstellensatz**: an ideal $I \subsetneq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$
  has $V(I) \ne \emptyset$.
- **Strong Nullstellensatz**: $I(V(I)) = \sqrt I$ (above).
- **Maximal ideals**: every maximal ideal of $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$
  has form $(x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n)$. Maximal ideals ↔ points.

## The dictionary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{algebra side} & \quad &\leftrightarrow & \quad &\text{geometry side} \\
&\text{ideals } I & & & &\text{varieties } V(I) \\
&\text{prime ideals} & & & &\text{irreducible varieties} \\
&\text{maximal ideals} & & & &\text{points} \\
&\text{radical of ideal} & & & &\text{vanishing ideal of }V(I)
\end{aligned}
$$

A *bijection* between radical ideals in $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ and
algebraic subsets of $k^n$ — the **algebra-geometry dictionary**.

## Worked example

In $\mathbb{C}[x, y]$:

- $V(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$ = unit circle.
- $V(xy)$ = union of $x = 0$ and $y = 0$ lines.
- $V(x^2)$ = "double" $y$-axis. Geometrically just $\{x = 0\}$, but
  $I(V(x^2)) = (x) \ne (x^2)$. The ideal "remembers"
  multiplicity; the variety doesn't.

Radical: $\sqrt{(x^2)} = (x)$. ✓ Confirms Nullstellensatz.

## Symbolic

**Algorithmic NSS**: given an ideal $I \subset \mathbb{Q}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$,
testing $V(I) = \emptyset$ over $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ reduces to checking
whether $1 \in I$ — Gröbner-basis computation.

**Effective Nullstellensatz**: bounds (Brownawell, Kollár) on the
degree $D$ such that $f^D \in I$ when $f$ vanishes on $V(I)$.

**Real Nullstellensatz**: over $\mathbb{R}$, the dictionary changes;
SOS (sum-of-squares) decompositions appear. Foundation of polynomial
optimisation (SOSTOOLS, Lasserre hierarchy).

**Stellensätze for inequalities**: Krivine, Schmüdgen, Putinar
positivstellensatz used in optimisation and verification.

## Computational

```python
import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols("x y")

# Compute radical of ideal generated by x^2
print(sp.radsimp(sp.simplify(x**2)))     # x² (just the polynomial)

# Verify NSS via Groebner basis
def is_consistent(F, vars):
    G = sp.groebner(F, vars)
    return 1 not in [g for g in G]

print(is_consistent([x**2 + y**2 - 1, x - 0.5], [x, y]))  # True
print(is_consistent([x**2 + 1, x**2 - 1], [x]))           # over R: [True/False?]
# In Q[x], x^2+1 and x^2-1 are coprime → 1 in ideal → not consistent
print(sp.gcdex(x**2 + 1, x**2 - 1, x))   # gives Bezout, so 1 in ideal

# Radical computation in Sage (pseudocode):
# I = ideal(x^2)
# I.radical()  → ideal(x)
```

## Applied

- **Polynomial system solvers** — compute $V(I)$ via Gröbner basis,
  with NSS guaranteeing the algorithm finds all complex solutions.
- **Robotics** — kinematic chains as polynomial systems; NSS-based
  techniques solve them.
- **Computer-aided design (CAD)** — surface intersection, offsets
  use polynomial-ideal manipulations.
- **Algebraic statistics** — model identifiability questions phrased
  as ideal-membership.
- **Cryptanalysis** — algebraic attacks on ciphers reduce key recovery
  to system solving.
